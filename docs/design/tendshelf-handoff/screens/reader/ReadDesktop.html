The Second Lamp

The second lamp had not been lit in eleven winters. Nell found it where her father always said it would be, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the stairs, its brass gone the soft brown of an old coin.

She carried it up the hundred and nine steps with both hands, the way you carry something that might still be asleep. At the top the wind was waiting. It leaned its whole weight on the glass and hummed through the frame, a low note that never quite resolved.

“You’ll want the long matches,” said a voice behind her, and she did not turn around, because she already knew whose it was.

Ivo had rowed out again. His coat was dark with spray to the elbows, and he held the matchbox out on a flat palm, as if she were a horse that might bolt.

“Your father kept them in the log drawer,” he said. “Second from the top. He told me once, in case.”

In case of what, she wanted to ask. Instead she took the box, struck a match against the iron rail, and cupped the small flame in both hands until it stopped shivering.

The wick took slowly. Light climbed the inside of the glass like water filling a jar, and for a moment the whole lantern room turned the colour of honey.

Below them, far out past the reef, something answered.
